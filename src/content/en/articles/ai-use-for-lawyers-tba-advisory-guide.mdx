Artificial intelligence tools are in practical use at many stages of legal practice, from statute and case-law research to document review, from contract analysis to drafting. The decisive question for the profession in light of this development is not whether AI will be used, but within which limits, safeguards and professional standards it will be used.

The "Advisory Guide on the Use of Artificial Intelligence by Lawyers" (2026), prepared by the Information and Technology Law Commission of the Union of Turkish Bar Associations (TBA), offers a principle-based framework for this question. The Guide builds on the work that emerged at the "Artificial Intelligence and the Legal Profession Workshop" held in Ankara on 14-15 February 2026. Its basic approach is summed up in one sentence: A lawyer may benefit from AI, but may at no stage delegate professional secrecy, personal data, the client's interests, the final legal judgment or professional responsibility to AI or to third parties.

This article addresses the Guide's core principles, its risk-based classification of uses, and its consequences for lawyers and clients.

## 1. Nature, Purpose and Scope of the Guide

The Guide's purpose is to set out the principles, limits and practical criteria that allow lawyers, trainee lawyers and persons working in law offices to use AI tools in line with their professional obligations. Its scope covers generative AI systems, large language models, document review tools, case-law and legislation search systems, contract analysis software, transcription and summarisation tools, client communication support systems and AI-supported solutions used in office management.

The Guide is to be interpreted so as to be consistent with the Attorneyship Law, the TBA Rules of Professional Conduct, the regulations on the legal profession, principles of disciplinary law and Law No. 6698 on the Protection of Personal Data (KVKK). It takes a technology-neutral approach and does not aim to endorse or prohibit any particular brand, product or provider. From its publication date it is treated as advisory and will be reviewed by the TBA where necessary.

## 2. Core Principles

The Guide lists the core principles to be observed in the use of AI as follows:

- **Human oversight and ultimate responsibility:** AI output cannot replace professional judgment; the final legal assessment, decision and responsibility belong to the lawyer.
- **Professional secrecy and confidentiality:** Client secrecy and file confidentiality are protected absolutely, regardless of any use of AI.
- **Data minimisation:** Only necessary, proportionate, lawful and, as far as possible, anonymised data is entered into an AI tool.
- **Compliance with law and professional rules:** Use must be compatible with the advertising ban, the ban on conflicts of interest, and the duties of care and loyalty.
- **Verification:** Every output concerning legislation, case law, time limits, evidence, calculations and material facts is confirmed against official and reliable sources.
- **Transparency and informing the client:** The client is informed where a use affects the client's rights, data or the outcome of the service.
- **Choosing reliable tools:** Tools are selected after examining data transfer, data location, security, reuse in model training, record keeping and contractual safeguards.
- **Equality and non-discrimination:** The possibility of bias and discriminatory results in outputs is critically screened, particularly in criminal, family, employment and immigration law.
- **Record keeping and accountability:** The purpose, the tool used, the date and the verification result of critical uses are recorded in the file.
- **Training and competence:** Lawyers, trainees and office staff are not directed to critical uses before being trained on the limits and risks of AI.

## 3. Risk-Based Classification of Use

Rather than subjecting AI use to uniform rules, the Guide provides for a risk-based classification. The classification takes into account together the type of data, the effect on the client, proximity to a legal outcome, whether the tool is an open or closed system, and the likelihood of transfer abroad. The Guide defines a "closed system" as one in which data is not included in third-party training datasets and not shared with third parties, access and records can be audited, and contractual and technical safeguards exist.

## 4. Uses to Be Avoided

The Guide groups uses that should, as a rule, be considered prohibited or incompatible with the nature of the legal profession under seven headings:

- **Uses contrary to client secrecy and personal data rules:** Non-anonymised client data, file contents, evidence, special categories of personal data, trade secrets or strategic negotiation information must not be entered into publicly available tools or tools with inadequate contractual safeguards. UYAP, court decisions and enforcement file contents must not be transferred to such tools without authorisation and without anonymisation.
- **Uses that remove human oversight:** Unreviewed output cannot be used as a final legal opinion; it cannot be submitted to the client, a court, the administration, an arbitrator, a mediator or third parties without verification.
- **Uses contrary to the duty of honesty towards judicial authorities:** Fabricating, or knowingly using, non-existent case law, legislation, sources or facts may give rise to legal and professional liability.
- **Uses contrary to the advertising ban and the lawyers' monopoly:** On the ground that AI is used, misleading statements such as "guaranteed result", "certain-win analysis", "predicts the court's outcome", "legal advice without humans" or "the fastest case resolution" cannot be made.
- **Uses carrying risks of discrimination, profiling and manipulation:** Profiling or scoring members of the judiciary using their identity data, or trying to predict how they will decide in a particular file, is regarded as problematic for professional activity.
- **Uses problematic for fees:** The time saved by AI cannot be invoiced as if it were time actually spent by the lawyer; the office's ordinary technology costs cannot be passed on to the client without prior information and agreement.
- **Uses problematic in legal technology development:** File contents cannot be shared for training, testing or developing AI systems without client consent and the necessary legal safeguards.

## 5. Professional Secrecy, Anonymisation and KVKK Compliance

According to the Guide, the lawyer's duty of confidentiality does not disappear or weaken because data is entered into an AI tool. Even if the client's name is not disclosed, the client may become identifiable through the event and its distinguishing elements. Anonymisation therefore does not consist only of deleting names and ID numbers; the date of the event, place, amount, company name, type of case, network of relationships and unique facts must also be assessed together.

In every use involving personal data, the purpose of processing must be determined, necessity and proportionality assessed, and the duty to inform and the legal ground examined. Because cloud-based language model services mostly process data on infrastructure abroad, input containing personal data cannot be used without assessing, under Article 9 of the KVKK, an adequacy decision, appropriate safeguards or incidental cases. A data processing agreement must be made with the service provider; sub-processors, retention periods and whether data will be reused in model training must be clearly regulated. In each use, whether the lawyer or office and the service provider are a data controller or a data processor must also be determined according to the specific case.

The Guide describes criminal files, files concerning children, family law, health data, asylum and migration files, trade secrets and capital markets transactions as highly sensitive areas. In these areas, closed, auditable systems with contractual safeguards and a defined data location should be preferred; if these conditions cannot be met, use should be limited to general research and anonymous topic planning.

## 6. Human Oversight and the Duty of Verification

The Guide emphasises that AI output cannot replace the lawyer's professional assessment. Every output used in a petition, opinion, contract, notice or client briefing must be checked for legal accuracy, fit with the facts, evidentiary position, time limits, procedural provisions, jurisdiction and competence, and current legislation and case law. The verification steps the Guide provides for are:

- **Legislation verification:** Article numbers, in-force status, date of amendment and applicability are checked against official sources.
- **Case-law verification:** File and decision numbers, date, chamber or board, outcome of the decision and similarity of facts are verified from an independent source.
- **Fact verification:** Every event, date, person, amount and item of evidence that the AI adds is compared with the contents of the file.
- **Logic and strategy review:** The output is assessed for fit with the client's interests, case strategy, burden of proof and procedural economy.
- **Second eye:** In high-risk files, a check is carried out by another lawyer.

The Guide lists hallucination, confirmation bias and automation bias among the ordinary risks of AI use, and states that a tool's confident tone cannot be accepted as an indicator of accuracy. If output containing fabricated case law or incorrect legislation is used, responsibility lies with the lawyer; using AI does not reduce the duty of care, on the contrary it increases the duty of oversight and verification.

## 7. Honesty towards Judicial Authorities

A lawyer cannot submit to a court, arbitrator, mediator or administrative authority any provision, precedent, reference or material fact obtained from an AI tool without confirming it from an official and reliable source. According to the Guide, submitting fabricated case law or references may, depending on the specifics of the case, be regarded as a serious breach of the duty of professional care. If the judicial authority or the applicable procedural rules require the use of AI to be disclosed, this disclosure must be made; even where there is no disclosure obligation, the lawyer is personally responsible for the accuracy of the content submitted. If it is later noticed that an erroneous output has entered the file, informing the authority concerned and the possibilities for correction must be assessed without delay.

## 8. Client Rights, Information and Fees

According to the Guide, where AI use remains at the level of general research, anonymous topic planning or administrative support and no client data is processed, detailed consent may not be required. By contrast, where client data, file contents, legal strategy, special categories of data or substantive text to be produced on the client's behalf are involved, the client must be informed and, where necessary, an explicit consent or approval mechanism must be operated.

A client may request that AI not be used in the client's file because of reasonable data security concerns. The lawyer assesses this request together with professional independence and the requirements of the work; if the request makes it impossible to provide the service properly, the lawyer may consider declining the matter or ending the power-of-attorney relationship in accordance with the legislation.

As to fees, the Guide states that benefiting from AI is not a ground for an automatic discount on the attorney's fee. The value of the service rests not on the speed of the tool used but on legal knowledge, responsibility, strategy, risk management and professional care. On the other hand, ordinary technology costs cannot be passed on to the client separately and in an unclear manner; extraordinary client-specific costs can arise only through prior information and a fee-and-expense agreement.

## 9. Selecting Tools and Assessing the Service Provider

The Guide recommends that tool selection should not rest only on ease of use or cost, and that a tool be assessed for professional secrecy, data security, accuracy, auditability and contractual safeguards. The main questions to put to the service provider in this context are:

- In which country and on what infrastructure is the data processed and stored?
- Are prompts, documents and outputs used for model training or product development?
- Who are the sub-processors, and is notice given when they change?
- What are the options for data deletion, export, record keeping and audit trails?
- What are the notification period and the liability regime in the event of a personal data breach?
- Which appropriate safeguard mechanism is used for transfer abroad?
- Are the legal data sources up to date, lawful and verifiable?

The Guide also reminds that accreditation or a compliance assessment does not mean a tool is absolutely safe or error-free. For sensitive files, instead of giving client data directly to the model as training data, approaches based on keeping it in a closed knowledge repository under the office's control and retrieving it at query time (RAG-like), as well as closed or locally running systems with a defined data location, should be preferred.

## 10. Copyright and Ownership of Output

A lawyer cannot give works owned or kept secret by third parties, doctrinal texts or licensed database content to an AI tool as input in breach of licence terms. The originality of the output and its non-infringement of third-party rights are the lawyer's responsibility. The input-output ownership, licence and reuse provisions in the tool's terms of use should be reviewed at the contracting stage.

## 11. Training, Office Policy and Incident Response

The Guide regards AI literacy as one of the current competence areas of the legal profession and recommends that AI literacy, ethical use, data security and verification skills be included in trainee education and in-service training. Trainee lawyers and office staff should not use AI in files containing client data without the lawyer's explicit instruction and supervision.

Every law office is advised to adopt a written AI use policy suited to its size. This policy should cover the list of approved tools, prohibited data types, the allocation of authority, records of critical uses, verification steps, the procedure for informing clients and notification of data breaches. If it is discovered that an erroneous output has been submitted to a court or a client, the responsible lawyer must be notified immediately, the effect on the file assessed, a correction made if possible and, where necessary, the client informed.

## 12. Professional Liability Insurance

The Guide recommends that lawyers check whether their existing professional liability insurance policies cover the risks of erroneous AI output, fabricated case law or references, and data breaches. It notes that where a policy is silent on this, coverage may be disputed, and recommends requesting written clarity from the insurer so that the scope is stated expressly. According to the Guide, insurance does not replace verification and safe-use measures; it complements them.

## Conclusion

The TBA Advisory Guide sets out a principle-based framework that does not prohibit AI but aims at its safe, conscious use in line with professional rules. The essence of its approach is clear: AI is a tool that supports the lawyer's professional activity, while the duty of professional secrecy, the protection of personal data, legal judgment and final professional responsibility remain with the lawyer under all circumstances. Although the Guide is advisory, the obligations it rests on arise from the Attorneyship Law, the professional rules and the KVKK, so it serves as a guiding standard of practice for law offices.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can lawyers use AI tools?

Yes. The TBA Guide does not prohibit AI; it treats it as a tool that supports the lawyer's professional activity. However, professional secrecy, personal data, the client's interests, the final legal judgment and professional responsibility cannot be delegated to AI or to third parties at any stage.

### Is the TBA AI Guide binding?

The Guide is advisory. However, the obligations it contains, such as confidentiality, care, honesty and the protection of personal data, arise from the Attorneyship Law, the professional rules and the KVKK. The Guide also states that the mere use of AI should not be treated as a ground for disciplinary sanction, and that the assessment should be made on the basis of a concrete breach, harm, fault or lack of care.

### Can client information be entered into AI tools?

Non-anonymised client information, special categories of personal data, file contents, evidence, trade secrets or strategic negotiation information must not be entered into publicly available tools or tools with inadequate contractual safeguards. Such data may be processed only if safeguards such as a closed system, written authorisation, KVKK review, analysis of transfer abroad, access limits and record keeping are in place.

### Can a client ask that AI not be used in the file?

Yes. A client may make this request because of reasonable data security concerns. The lawyer assesses the request together with professional independence and the requirements of the work; if the request makes it impossible to provide the service properly, the lawyer may consider declining the matter or ending the power-of-attorney relationship in accordance with the legislation.

### Does using AI reduce the attorney's fee?

No. According to the Guide, benefiting from AI is not a ground for an automatic fee discount; the value of the service rests on the lawyer's legal judgment, responsibility and care. However, the time saved by AI cannot be invoiced as if it were time actually spent by the lawyer, and ordinary technology costs cannot be passed on to the client without prior agreement.

### Who is responsible for an error in AI output?

The lawyer. According to the Guide, responsibility for using output containing fabricated case law, incorrect legislation or a wrong legal conclusion lies with the lawyer. Every output must therefore be verified against official and reliable sources for legislation, case law, facts and strategy before it is submitted to the client, a court or third parties.
