Turkey's Personal Data Protection Authority (KVKK) has published the *Practice Guide on the Protection of Personal Data in the Professional Activities of Lawyers*, prepared with the views and contributions of the Union of Turkish Bar Associations (TBB) (KVKK Publications No. 115, July 2026). The Guide was presented to the profession on 22 September 2026 at an event organised jointly by the Authority and the TBB.

Its starting point is to balance three core guarantees of legal practice, namely professional secrecy, the right of defence and the right to a fair trial, with the right to protection of personal data. As stressed at the launch, the Guide does not tell lawyers to stop processing data. Its message is: process the data your work requires, on the correct legal ground, only as far as needed and securely.

Below we summarise the Guide's key principles together with the Board decisions it relies on.

> **In short**
>
> - Legal practice is subject to the KVKK; the judicial exception does not cover lawyers.
> - A lawyer is, as a rule, a data controller. For client data, the basis is a privacy notice, not explicit consent.
> - Uploading documents containing personal data to AI tools may count as a data transfer.

## Is legal practice within the scope of the KVKK?

Yes. Article 28(1)(d) of Law No. 6698 excludes from the Law's scope processing carried out by judicial authorities or enforcement offices in connection with investigation, prosecution, trial and enforcement.

The Guide stresses that this exception requires two conditions together: the processing must relate to a judicial or enforcement process, and it must be carried out by a judicial authority or enforcement office. Judicial authorities means bodies established by the Constitution such as the courts, prosecutors' offices and the Constitutional Court. Board decision no. 2020/26 of 14 January 2020 takes the same view.

Since a lawyer is not a judicial authority, processing during professional activities does not benefit from the exception. Lawyers are subject to Law No. 6698.

## Is a lawyer a data controller or a data processor?

According to the Guide, a lawyer is as a rule a data controller. The reason is the independence of the profession: even if the client gives instructions, the lawyer ultimately decides which data is processed, for what purpose and by what means. The Guide notes that comparative law takes the same approach.

What matters is the actual situation, not the label in the contract. In decision no. 2023/437 of 22 March 2023, the Board held that a law partnership described as a processor in its contract was a controller because it in fact directed the processing.

The status must still be assessed case by case. The Guide also cites a Board decision (2023/78) in which a law partnership that sent a single notification message under limited instructions, without any means of verifying the number, was treated as a processor.

## On what legal ground does a lawyer process data?

According to the Guide, lawyers' processing mainly relies on the following grounds:

- **Client data:** Processing is directly related to the formation or performance of the engagement agreement (Art. 5(2)(c)).
- **Data of the opposing party and third parties:** Processing is necessary to establish, exercise or protect a right (Art. 5(2)(e)); it is expressly provided for by laws such as the Code of Civil Procedure, the Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law and the Attorneys' Act (Art. 5(2)(a)).
- **Statutory tasks such as issuing fee receipts:** The controller's legal obligation (Art. 5(2)(ç)).

### Is client consent required?

This is the question asked most often in practice, and the Guide's answer is clear. Explicit consent is only one of the legal grounds. It is used where no other ground exists or where the law specifically requires it. It is not a "just in case" basis to be added alongside other grounds.

Asking for explicit consent where another legal ground applies may create a false expectation in the data subject and may be incompatible with good faith. The Guide gives an example on this point.

The practical result: in client relationships, a privacy notice explaining the purposes and legal grounds of processing, rather than a consent form, is consistent with the Guide's approach.

## Special category data

Health, trade union membership, criminal convictions, political opinions... In legal practice it is almost impossible not to encounter such data. The Guide accepts that special category data may also be processed, to the extent the dispute requires, on the ground of establishing, exercising or protecting a right.

In decision no. 2021/925, the Board found no breach where such information was used in the statement of claim of a reinstatement case alleging dismissal for union activity.

A reminder: whenever special category data is processed, the adequate measures set out in Board decision no. 2018/10 of 31 January 2018 must be taken.

## How can a lawyer obtain personal data?

**Requests for information and documents under Art. 2 of the Attorneys' Act.** Judicial bodies, public institutions, banks, notaries, insurance companies and foundations are obliged to assist lawyers in performing their duties. The Guide accepts that this obligation is a lawful basis for those institutions to transfer data. Searches made through the UYAP and TAKPAS systems within the legal framework were also found lawful. Referring to Council of State case law, the Guide considers that explaining the link between the request and the matter is consistent with proportionality.

**Special statutory rules.** Art. 2 of the Attorneys' Act applies "without prejudice to special provisions in other laws". The Board held, for example, that access to criminal records is governed by the special rules of the Criminal Records Law (2021/1111).

**Health data.** Article 10 of the Regulation on Personal Health Data, which required a special authority in the power of attorney for lawyers, was repealed by an amendment of 3 December 2025. This makes access to clients' health records easier in practice.

**Lookup software.** Under the Board's principle decision no. 2019/308 of 18 October 2019, software and applications that allow identity and contact searches over unlawfully obtained data must not be used.

**Publicly available sources.** Data that the data subject has personally made public may be processed on the ground of publication by the data subject. The Guide requires two conditions: the person must have intended to make the data public, and the use must be consistent with that purpose. Obtaining information on a company officer from the trade registry is one of the Guide's examples.

## Disclosing data to third parties

Disclosing personal data is itself processing and must rely on one of the conditions in the Law. In Board decisions, sharing debt information with relatives who were not part of the collection process was found unlawful.

By contrast, sending garnishment notices to third parties under Art. 89 of the Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law (2021/909, 2021/1069), and contacting the person who signed the attachment report and was appointed custodian about the process (2022/1281), were found lawful.

The test is simple: does the disclosure rest on a legal process or the protection of a right, and is it proportionate?

## AI tools and data transfers

**Transfers within Turkey.** Transfers a lawyer makes on the client's behalf to courts, enforcement offices and mediators are assessed under Article 8 of the Law and its general principles.

**Transfers abroad.** Since the 2024 amendment by Law No. 7499, transfers abroad rely first on an adequacy decision. Without one, an appropriate safeguard such as standard contracts, binding corporate rules or an undertaking is required. A standard contract must be notified to the Authority within five business days of signature. Where no safeguard can be provided, only the exceptions listed in the Law for incidental transfers are available.

**Generative AI tools.** This is perhaps the Guide's most topical finding. Uploading a document containing personal data to a generative AI tool is not just getting technical help; it may also be a data transfer. If the provider or servers are abroad, Article 9 applies; if in Turkey, Article 8. The Guide recommends:

- Mask or anonymise personal data as far as possible.
- Do not upload special category data to these tools.
- Share only the minimum data needed for the purpose.
- Review the tool's data retention, model training, human review and sub-processor policies.
- Adopt written usage rules within the firm.

The same assessment should be made for email, cloud storage and booking systems with servers abroad. These tools are often what firms overlook.

## A lawyer's obligations as data controller

### VERBİS registration

Lawyers were exempted from registration with the Data Controllers' Registry (VERBİS) by Board decision no. 2018/32 of 2 April 2018. As the Guide stresses, the exemption covers only registration and notification; all other obligations under the Law continue.

### Privacy notices

Where data is collected from the data subject, notice is given at the time of collection. Where data comes from the client or public bodies, notice should be given at the first contact with the data subject, or at the latest at the first transfer if one is made.

The Guide cites layered notice as good practice: a short message through an automated greeting when someone calls, with a link to the full notice on the website; collecting only identity and contact details at first; and providing the written notice before the face-to-face meeting. The [contact form](/en/contact) on this site follows the same approach: a short notice above the form, the [full text](/en/privacy-notice) on a separate page.

### Retention and destruction

Erasure requests are assessed by applying Article 7 of the Law together with Article 39 of the Attorneys' Act. Under Art. 39, a lawyer must keep documents entrusted to them for three years after the mandate ends; if the client has been notified in writing to collect them, the period ends three months after the notice. If a request is refused, the reasons and retention period must be communicated to the data subject.

### Data subject requests

Requests are concluded as soon as possible according to their nature, and at the latest within thirty days, free of charge. The Guide notes that a significant share of complaints reaching the Board concern where the data was obtained. Recording the source of each item of data (client, enforcement file, public body or public source) therefore makes answering a request much easier. Keeping contact details on the website and the bar sign up to date is also among the Guide's recommendations.

### Breach notification

If personal data is obtained by others unlawfully, the Board must be notified within 72 hours of discovery and the data subjects within the shortest reasonable time (Board decision no. 2019/10). The Guide gives the loss of a physical case file as an example to be assessed in this light.

### Data security

Technical and organisational measures should match the firm's structure and risks, taking into account the Authority's Personal Data Security Guide. According to the Guide, data security is an ancillary obligation arising from the engagement agreement. Some recommendations are very concrete: protecting physical files in public areas and not writing party details on file covers.

For breach of data security obligations, the revalued administrative fine range for 2026 is TRY 256,357 to TRY 17,092,242.

## Checklist for your firm

- Use a privacy notice with clients; rely on explicit consent only where no other ground exists.
- Identify the controller according to how you practise (independently, jointly, as a partnership).
- In substitution (tevkil) arrangements, define the purpose and scope of shared data.
- Record the source of the data you process.
- Observe special statutory rules for data subject to them.
- Obtain data only from lawful sources and by lawful means.
- In debt collection, share information only within the legal process and proportionately.
- Put internal rules for AI, email and cloud services in writing.
- Apply the measures in decision no. 2018/10 for special category data.
- Set retention periods together with Art. 39 of the Attorneys' Act; answer requests within thirty days.
- Keep contact details on your website and bar sign up to date.
- Create an internal data breach procedure and inform your staff.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do lawyers have to register with VERBİS?

No. Lawyers are exempt under Board decision no. 2018/32. According to the Guide, the exemption does not extend to the other obligations under Law No. 6698.

### Is client consent required?

As a rule, no. Client data is processed in performing the engagement agreement. Explicit consent is used where no other legal ground exists.

### How should a request from the opposing party about their data be answered?

Within thirty days at the latest. Where the data was obtained, in which legal process it is processed and to whom it was transferred should be explained as appropriate to the request.

### Is using AI tools a data transfer?

It can be. Uploading documents containing personal data may be a domestic or cross-border transfer depending on where the provider and servers are located. The Guide recommends masking data and adopting internal rules.

## Final word

The Guide takes account of the particular nature of the legal profession and places the core tools of legal practice, namely the power to request information and documents under the Attorneys' Act, UYAP searches and notices in enforcement proceedings, on a clear legal footing together with Board decisions.

One point the Guide underlines is worth keeping in mind: because the concept of personal data is wider than secrecy, the duty to protect personal data is a broader framework that also encompasses the duty of professional secrecy. Turning the Guide into a checklist and building it into the firm's daily workflow also serves to preserve client trust.
